Recommended study plan

  1. Scan the learning outcomes below (and the linked midterm study guides). Mark each as: confident / shaky / blank.
  2. Rework Unit I and Unit II with the midterm packets. Use Midterm 1 study guide / practice and Midterm 2 study guide / practice for fluency you have already practiced once.
  3. Go through Lectures 35–40 and Labs 5–6 for material since Midterm 2 (eigenvectors, complex numbers, least squares, SVD concepts). Match shaky outcomes to fill-ins, worked examples, and Check yourself items.
  4. Build a cumulative “important definitions and formulas” list. Include vectors and geometry, matrices / \(Ax\) / systems, dynamics, eigenpairs, least-squares residual language, and SVD rank-\(k\) ideas — not only the newest topics.
  5. Rework quiz and lab styles from the whole term (Quizzes 1–10; Labs 0–6 ideas). Cover solutions, redo by hand, then check filled-in notes in Canvas.
  6. Start memorizing your list.
  7. Try the final practice packet with no notes — Sections A–C by unit, then Section D mixed.
  8. Check answers (end of the practice packet). For every miss, update your list and redo a similar lecture or midterm-practice item.
  9. Memorize the final list so you can write it from memory before the exam. In class you will not have notes.

Unit I — Vectors and geometry (Lectures 01–14)

Use the Midterm 1 study guide for the full outcome list. High-priority skills for the final:

  1. Represent data as vectors; add, scale, and form linear combinations.
  2. Compute and interpret inner products, norms, distances, RMS, angles, and cosine similarity.
  3. Distinguish linear vs affine models; evaluate \(a^{\mathsf{T}}x+b\) in context.
  4. Run a small nearest-neighbor or \(k\)-means step by hand and discuss scale.
  5. Use dependence, span, subspace, basis, dimension, and orthonormal language accurately.

Unit II — Matrices, systems, and dynamics (Lectures 18–32)

Use the Midterm 2 study guide for the full outcome list. High-priority skills for the final:

  1. Compute \(Ax\), interpret columns, and recognize common 2D transforms.
  2. Translate contexts into \(Ax=b\); solve small systems; read RREF (pivots, free variables, consistency).
  3. Model \(x_{t+1}=Ax_t\); compute a few steps; relate \(A^k x\) to long-run behavior.
  4. Multiply matrices; compose transforms; compare computational orders.
  5. Use invertibility, rank, column space, null space, and \(2\times 2\) / triangular determinants.

Unit III — Since Midterm 2 (Lectures 35–40)

Lecture 35 — Eigenvectors as persistent directions

  1. Explain geometrically what makes \(v\) an eigenvector.
  2. Verify a proposed eigenpair \((A,v,\lambda)\) by checking \(Av=\lambda v\).
  3. Find \(\lambda\) when an eigenvector is given.
  4. Connect eigenvectors to repeated transformations and steady directions.
  5. Recognize that a rotation may have no real eigenvector, motivating complex numbers.

Lecture 36 — Characteristic equation, \(2\times 2\) eigenproblems, and complex numbers

  1. Interpret \(a+bi\) algebraically and as a point/vector in the complex plane.
  2. Perform the basic complex arithmetic needed for eigenvalue calculations.
  3. Form and solve \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\) for a \(2\times 2\) matrix.
  4. Find corresponding eigenvectors (including simple complex cases at the course’s level).
  5. Check real or complex eigenpairs by substitution.

Lecture 37 — Why did the system behave that way? (+ Lab 5 / Quiz 10)

  1. Compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors with software and verify \(Av\approx\lambda v\).
  2. Use dominant eigenbehavior to explain growth, decay, or approach to a direction.
  3. Interpret complex eigenvalues as rotation / oscillation behavior.
  4. Observe orthogonal eigenvectors for a symmetric example when it appears.
  5. Connect eigen-language back to earlier dynamical simulations.

Lecture 38 — When \(Ax=b\) has no exact solution

  1. Recognize an inconsistent overdetermined system.
  2. Formulate the least-squares objective \(\min_x \lVert Ax-b\rVert\).
  3. Interpret the fitted vector \(\hat{b}=A\hat{x}\) as the closest attainable vector in \(C(A)\).
  4. Interpret the residual \(r=b-A\hat{x}\) geometrically.
  5. Distinguish an exact solution from a best approximate solution.

Lecture 39 — Least squares, data fitting, and positive definiteness (+ Lab 6)

  1. Compute a least-squares solution with software (and check a tiny case by hand when asked).
  2. Interpret residuals and RMS error in context.
  3. Check that the residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(A\) (normal equations idea).
  4. Recognize that \(A^{\mathsf{T}}A\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, and positive definite when \(A\) has independent columns.
  5. Evaluate whether a fitted model is useful, not only report coefficients.

Lecture 40 — SVD, low-rank approximation, and capstone launch

  1. Interpret \(A=U\Sigma V^{\mathsf{T}}\) conceptually (orthogonal factors + singular values).
  2. Explain what a rank-\(k\) approximation keeps and what it discards.
  3. Compare singular-value decay qualitatively for different data matrices.
  4. Relate reconstruction error and storage to the choice of rank \(k\).
  5. State a practical limitation of a low-rank model (what structure it may miss).

Cumulative connections (what the final emphasizes)

Be ready to choose a tool and explain why, not only compute in isolation:

Situation Tools that often help
Compare or cluster feature vectors Norm, distance, angle / cosine, nearest neighbor, \(k\)-means
“Is \(b\) attainable exactly?” Column space, RREF consistency, rank
“All solutions / free variables” RREF, null space
“Undo a reversible transform” Inverse / unique solution of \(Ax=b\)
“Where is the state after many steps?” \(A^k x\), eigenvalues / dominant direction
“Best approximate fit when overdetermined” Least squares, residual, RMS
“Compress / approximate a matrix or image” SVD / rank-\(k\) idea
“Hand vs software” Small \(2\times 2\) / \(3\times 3\) by hand; larger or SVD/eigen via software interpretation
