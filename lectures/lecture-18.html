Lecture 18 — Matrices as data and structured objects

Mon Oct 5

Modified

October 5, 2026

Download PDF

Last updated: October 5, 2026.

Reading: Boyd 6.1–6.3. Related objectives: Lectures. Schedule.

Bring paper. Warm-up while attendance is taken, then fill-in notes, board examples, and Your turn.

Learning objectives

By the end of class, students should be able to:

  1. Identify matrix dimensions, rows, columns, and entries.
  2. Represent datasets, networks, images, or systems as matrices.
  3. Perform matrix addition, scaling, and transposition.
  4. Recognize zero, identity, diagonal, triangular, symmetric, and sparse matrices.
  5. Explain one practical consequence of matrix structure.

Warm-up

Let \[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

  1. What are the dimensions of \(A\)? Is it tall, wide, or square?

  2. What is \(A_{23}\)? What is the second column of \(A\) as a vector?

Fill in

An \(m\times n\) matrix is a rectangular array with \(\underline{\hspace{1.4cm}}\) rows and \(\underline{\hspace{1.4cm}}\) columns. The entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is written \(A_{ij}\) (Boyd: indices start at \(1\)).

We can view \(A\) as its \(n\) columns (each an \(m\)-vector) side by side, or as its \(m\) rows (each an \(n\)-row-vector) stacked.

Examples of “matrix as data”:

  • An image: pixel values in a grid.
  • A table: locations \(\times\) days, or customers \(\times\) products.
  • A relation / directed graph: adjacency matrix with \(A_{ij}=1\) when there is an edge from \(j\) to \(i\) (Boyd’s convention).

Transpose. \((A^{\mathsf{T}})_{ij}=\underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}\). If \(A\) is \(m\times n\), then \(A^{\mathsf{T}}\) is \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\).

Addition and scaling. \(A+B\) and \(\beta A\) are entrywise (same size required for addition).

Special matrices (square, unless noted):

Name Pattern
Zero \(0\) all entries \(\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\)
Identity \(I\) \(1\)s on the diagonal, \(0\)s elsewhere
Diagonal off-diagonal entries are \(\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\)
Upper triangular zeros \(\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}\) the diagonal
Symmetric \(A^{\mathsf{T}}=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\)
Sparse \(\underline{\hspace{2.8cm}}\) of the entries are nonzero

Practical consequence: structure (sparsity, triangularity, symmetry) often means cheaper storage or faster algorithms — we take advantage of the pattern instead of treating every entry as generic.

Worked in class

W1. Dimensions and pieces. Let \[ B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \] Give \(B_{31}\), the third row, and the first column. Is \(B\) square?

W2. Data matrix. Three weather stations record (high °F, low °F) for one day. Station 1: \((62,48)\); station 2: \((58,45)\); station 3: \((65,50)\). Write a \(2\times 3\) matrix \(W\) whose columns are the station readings. What does \(W_{12}\) mean?

W3. Arithmetic. With \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&-1\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\3&2\end{bmatrix}, \] compute \(A+C\), \(2A\), and \(A^{\mathsf{T}}\).

W4. Structure check. For each matrix, name every special type that applies (zero / identity / diagonal / triangular / symmetric / sparse — sparse is judgment call for tiny examples).

  1. \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\)

  2. \(\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&-3\end{bmatrix}\)

  3. \(\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\)

  4. \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix}\)

W5. Why structure matters. An \(n\times n\) identity matrix has how many nonzero entries? Density \(nnz/(n^2)\)? Why might software store only the nonzeros of a sparse customer-purchase matrix?

Your turn

Y1. Let \[ M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. \] Dimensions? \(M_{21}\)? Second column? Is \(M\) tall or wide?

Y2. A tiny directed graph on nodes \(\{1,2,3\}\) has edges \(2\to 1\), \(1\to 3\), and \(3\to 2\). Using Boyd’s convention (\(A_{ij}=1\) means edge from \(j\) to \(i\)), write the \(3\times 3\) adjacency matrix.

Y3. With \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\), compute \(A+A^{\mathsf{T}}\). Is the result symmetric? Why?

Y4. True or false, with a one-line reason: every diagonal matrix is symmetric.

NoteAnswers (Your turn only)

Y1. \(2\times 3\); wide. \(M_{21}=-1\). Second column \((2,3)\).

Y2. \[ A=\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \] (edge \(2\to 1\) puts a \(1\) in row 1, column 2; \(1\to 3\) puts a \(1\) in row 3, column 1; \(3\to 2\) puts a \(1\) in row 2, column 3).

Y3. \(A+A^{\mathsf{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&6\end{bmatrix}\), which equals its transpose, so yes — sum of a matrix and its transpose is always symmetric.

Y4. True. Off-diagonal entries are already \(0=0\), so \(A^{\mathsf{T}}=A\).

## Fill in

An *$m\times n$ matrix* is a rectangular array with $\underline{\hspace{1.4cm}}$ rows and $\underline{\hspace{1.4cm}}$ columns. The entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is written $A_{ij}$ (Boyd: indices start at $1$).

We can view $A$ as its $n$ **columns** (each an $m$-vector) side by side, or as its $m$ **rows** (each an $n$-row-vector) stacked.

Examples of “matrix as data”:

- An image: pixel values in a grid.
- A table: locations $\times$ days, or customers $\times$ products.
- A **relation / directed graph**: adjacency matrix with $A_{ij}=1$ when there is an edge from $j$ to $i$ (Boyd’s convention).

**Transpose.** $(A^{\mathsf{T}})_{ij}=\underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}$. If $A$ is $m\times n$, then $A^{\mathsf{T}}$ is $\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}$.

**Addition and scaling.** $A+B$ and $\beta A$ are entrywise (same size required for addition).

Special matrices (square, unless noted):

| Name | Pattern |
|------|---------|
| Zero $0$ | all entries $\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}$ |
| Identity $I$ | $1$s on the diagonal, $0$s elsewhere |
| Diagonal | off-diagonal entries are $\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}$ |
| Upper triangular | zeros $\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}$ the diagonal |
| Symmetric | $A^{\mathsf{T}}=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}$ |
| Sparse | $\underline{\hspace{2.8cm}}$ of the entries are nonzero |

Practical consequence: structure (sparsity, triangularity, symmetry) often means cheaper storage or faster algorithms — we take advantage of the pattern instead of treating every entry as generic.
