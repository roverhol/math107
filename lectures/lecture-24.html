Reading: Boyd 11.1–11.3; class notes + selected Strang §3.5 (rank, \(C(A)\), \(N(A)\)). Related objectives: Lectures. Schedule.

Monday continued Lab 3 (including the \([A\mid I]\) peek). Today names that right block \(A^{-1}\), then connects invertibility to the RREF language you already use: column space, null space, and rank. An inverse is the square case where every \(b\) has exactly one \(x\). Not a four-subspaces theory day.

Bring paper. Warm-up, fill-in notes, board examples, and Your turn.

Learning objectives

By the end of class, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why reducing \([A\mid I]\) to \([I\mid B]\) gives \(B=A^{-1}\), and use an inverse to solve \(Ax=b\).
  2. Interpret \(C(A)\) as the possible outputs \(Ax\) and \(N(A)\) as the inputs sent to zero.
  3. Find rank from pivots and relate it to free variables and \(\dim N(A)\).
  4. Explain when \(Ax=b\) is solvable and when a solution, if it exists, is unique.
  5. Distinguish square invertibility from full column rank for a tall matrix.

Warm-up

Recall \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(E=\begin{bmatrix}1&-2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\).

  1. What row operation does multiplying by \(E\) perform?

  2. Compute \(EA\).

  3. What does this tell us about solving \(Ax=b\)?

Fill in

Inverse from elementary matrices

If elementary matrices \(E_1,\ldots,E_k\) reduce a square matrix \(A\) to \(I\), then \[ E_k\cdots E_1A=I,\qquad A^{-1}=E_k\cdots E_1. \] Row-reducing \([A\mid I]\) to \([I\mid B]\) therefore gives \(B=\underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}\). The same matrix solves every right-hand side: if \(Ax=b\), then \(x=\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\).

Check. After you find a candidate \(A^{-1}\), multiply: \[ A^{-1}A=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}} \qquad\text{(and/or $AA^{-1}=I$)}. \] If the product is not \(I\), you made an arithmetic error — or \(A\) was not invertible.

Column space and null space

The column space \(C(A)\) is the span of the columns of \(A\) — every vector of the form \(Ax\). So \[ Ax=b\text{ is solvable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b\in\underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}. \]

The null space is \(N(A)=\{x:Ax=\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}\}\). If \(Ax_p=b\), every other solution has the form \(\underline{\hspace{3.2cm}}\). A solution, when one exists, is unique exactly when \(\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}\).

Rank from pivots

A pivot in \(\operatorname{rref}(A)\) is the leading \(1\) in a nonzero row. Pivot columns of \(A\) are the corresponding columns of the original matrix. Example: \[ \operatorname{rref}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2&0\\0&1&-3&0\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \] has pivots in columns \(\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}\); column \(\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}\) is free.

Basis / dimension (recall). A basis is a linearly independent spanning set; dimension is how many vectors sit in any basis.

Rank. \(\operatorname{rank}(A)=\dim C(A)=\) the \(\underline{\hspace{3.6cm}}\) of \(A\). For an \(m\times n\) matrix, \[ \dim N(A)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A) \qquad\text{(rank--nullity).} \]

Square case (Monday’s inverse, restated)

For \(n\times n\) matrices, \[ A\text{ invertible}\iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=n \iff C(A)=\mathbb R^n\iff N(A)=\{0\}. \] Rectangular matrices never have a two-sided inverse; we still use \(C(A)\), \(N(A)\), and rank. Later, least squares asks for the closest \(Ax\) when \(b\notin C(A)\).

Worked in class

Three \(2\times 2\) inverses

W1. Find \(A^{-1}\) by reducing \([A\mid I]\). Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] (a) Form \([A\mid I]\) and row-reduce to \([I\mid A^{-1}]\).
(b) Check: compute \(A^{-1}A\) and confirm you get \(I\).

W2. Another \(2\times 2\). Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}. \] (a) Find \(A^{-1}\) by reducing \([A\mid I]\).
(b) Check: compute \(A^{-1}A\) (should be \(I\)).
(c) Solve \(Ax=b\) for \(b=(5,13)^T\) using \(x=A^{-1}b\), then verify \(Ax=b\).

W3. When the inverse does not exist. Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}. \] (a) Try to reduce \([A\mid I]\) to \([I\mid ?]\). What goes wrong?
(b) Find a nonzero vector in \(N(A)\). If \(Ax=b\) has one solution, how many does it have?

W3b. General \(2\times 2\) formula. For \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}, \] row-reducing \([A\mid I]\) (when it works) always produces \[ A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}}\begin{bmatrix}\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}&\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}\\ \underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}&\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}\end{bmatrix}. \] The scalar in the denominator is \(ad-bc\) (the determinant of \(A\) — more Friday). If \(ad-bc=0\), there is no inverse (same failure as W3).

Quick check: apply the formula to W1 and W2 and match what you already found.

Column space, rank, and synthesis

W4. Column space. Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] Describe \(C(A)\). Is \(b=(1,2,3)^T\) in \(C(A)\)? Is \(b'=(1,3,0)^T\)?

W5. Rank from RREF. Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{rref}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}. \] What is \(\operatorname{rank}(A)\)? Give a basis vector for \(N(A)\).

W6. Synthesis. For an \(m\times n\) matrix, match: existence of a solution; uniqueness (when solvable); rank; when an inverse can exist.

Your turn

Y1. Find \(A^{-1}\) for \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) by reducing \([A\mid I]\). Check by computing \(A^{-1}A\).

Y2. For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}\), give one nonzero vector in \(N(A)\) and explain what it means for uniqueness.

Y3. For \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\2&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\), is \(b=(2,4,-1)^T\) in \(C(A)\)? Is \(b'=(2,5,-1)^T\)?

Y4. An \(A\in\mathbb R^{3\times2}\) has rank \(2\). Is \(N(A)=\{0\}\)? Does every \(b\in\mathbb R^3\) have a solution? If a solution exists, is it unique? Is \(A\) invertible?

NoteAnswers (Your turn only)

Y1. Row-reduce \([A\mid I]\) to get \[ A^{-1}=\frac16\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}. \] Check: \(A^{-1}A=I\).

Y2. \((2,-1)^T\) (or any nonzero multiple). Any consistent system has infinitely many solutions: add \(t(2,-1)^T\).

Y3. Yes — \(b\) with \(x=(2,-1)^T\). No — the second entry must equal twice the first.

Y4. Yes (\(N(A)=\{0\}\)); no (not every \(b\in\mathbb R^3\)); yes (unique when solvable); no, \(A\) is not square, so not invertible.

## Fill in

### Inverse from elementary matrices

If elementary matrices $E_1,\ldots,E_k$ reduce a square matrix $A$ to $I$, then
$$
E_k\cdots E_1A=I,\qquad A^{-1}=E_k\cdots E_1.
$$

**Check.** After you find a candidate $A^{-1}$, multiply:
$$
A^{-1}A=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}
\qquad\text{(and/or $AA^{-1}=I$)}.
$$
If the product is not $I$, you made an arithmetic error — or $A$ was not invertible.

### Column space and null space

The **column space** $C(A)$ is the span of the columns of $A$ — every vector of the form $Ax$. So
$$
Ax=b\text{ is solvable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b\in\underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}.
$$

The **null space** is $N(A)=\{x:Ax=\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}\}$. If $Ax_p=b$, every other solution has the form $\underline{\hspace{3.2cm}}$. A solution, when one exists, is unique exactly when $\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}$.

### Rank from pivots

A **pivot** in $\operatorname{rref}(A)$ is the leading $1$ in a nonzero row. Pivot columns of $A$ are the corresponding columns of the original matrix. Example:
$$
\operatorname{rref}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2&0\\0&1&-3&0\\0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
$$
has pivots in columns $\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}$; column $\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}}$ is free.

**Basis / dimension (recall).** A basis is a linearly independent spanning set; dimension is how many vectors sit in any basis.

**Rank.** $\operatorname{rank}(A)=\dim C(A)=$ the $\underline{\hspace{3.6cm}}$ of $A$. For an $m\times n$ matrix,
$$
\dim N(A)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A)
\qquad\text{(rank--nullity).}
$$

### Square case (Monday’s inverse, restated)

For $n\times n$ matrices,
$$
A\text{ invertible}\iff \operatorname{rank}(A)=n
\iff C(A)=\mathbb R^n\iff N(A)=\{0\}.
$$
