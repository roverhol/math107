Reading: Boyd 11.1–11.3; recall elementary matrices from Lecture 21. Related objectives: Lectures. Schedule. Lab 3 already peeked at \([A\mid I]\) for the nutrient matrix — today we name the right block \(A^{-1}\) and make the idea general.

Bring paper. Warm-up, fill-in notes, board examples, and Your turn.

Learning objectives

By the end of class, you should be able to:

  1. Explain \(A^{-1}\) as the product of elementary matrices that reduce \(A\) to \(I\).
  2. Derive and use the \(2\times 2\) inverse formula from elimination.
  3. Compute a \(3\times 3\) inverse by eliminating \([A\mid I]\) to \([I\mid A^{-1}]\) (when it exists).
  4. Check a candidate inverse with \(A^{-1}A=I\) (or \(AA^{-1}=I\)).
  5. Explain why \(A^{-1}\) is a general undo: the same matrix gives \(x=A^{-1}b\) for every \(b\).
  6. Recognize when no inverse exists, and connect invertibility to unique solvability of \(Ax=b\).

Warm-up

In Lab 3 you reduced \([A\mid I]\) for the nutrient matrix and got a right block \(B\) with \(BA=I\) and \(Bb=x\) for every meal target \(b\) you tried.

  1. What name should \(B\) get?

  2. In Lecture 21 we had \(E_3 E_2 E_1 A=I\). How does that product relate to \(B\)?

Fill in

Inverse from elementary matrices

A square matrix \(A\) is invertible if there exist elementary matrices \(E_1,\ldots,E_k\) (row operations) with \[ E_k\cdots E_2 E_1 A = \underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}. \] We then define \[ A^{-1} := E_k\cdots E_2 E_1. \] So \(A^{-1}A=I\). (When \(A\) is invertible one also gets \(AA^{-1}=I\).)

Meaning. Each \(E_i\) undoes / performs one reversible row operation. Their product undoes the whole map \(x\mapsto Ax\): if \(y=Ax\), then \(A^{-1}y=x\).

Solving — why the inverse is powerful. If \(A\) is invertible and \(Ax=b\), then \[ x = \underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}. \] The point of \(A^{-1}\) is generality: you build one matrix that works for any right-hand side. Change \(b\), keep the same \(A^{-1}\), multiply again. Equivalently: \(Ax=b\) has a unique solution for every \(b\) iff \(A\) is invertible.

(Elimination on \([A\mid b]\) still finds one \(x\) for one \(b\). The inverse is the packaged “undo everything” map.)

Same \(E_i\), two packages (Lab 3). Stopping elimination at an upper-triangular \(U\) gives a factorization \(A=LU\) — great for solving many systems with the same \(A\). Pushing all the way to \(I\) on \([A\mid I]\) builds \(A^{-1}\) — great for the undo map. Software’s A\b is closer to the LU idea than to forming \(A^{-1}\) first.

Practical computation

To find \(A^{-1}\) by elimination, form the wide matrix \([A\mid I]\) and row-reduce until the left block is \(I\) (when possible). The right block is then \(\underline{\hspace{1.4cm}}\).

For \(2\times 2\), that procedure yields a closed formula (next). For \(3\times 3\), do the elimination by hand on \([A\mid I]\).

Check. Always verify \(A^{-1}A=I\) (or \(AA^{-1}=I\)) on a candidate.

When it fails. If a zero row appears on the left while reducing \([A\mid I]\), you cannot reach \(I\): \(A\) has \(\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}\) inverse. (Columns are dependent; \(Ax=0\) has a nontrivial solution.)

Worked in class

W1. Product of \(E_i\) is the inverse. Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] (a) Write the elementary matrix \(E\) that subtracts \(2\) times row 2 from row 1 (after you already have a pivot \(1\) in position \((2,2)\) — or clear the \((1,2)\) entry of \(A\) directly). Check \(EA=I\).

  1. Conclude \(A^{-1}=E\) and write \(A^{-1}\) explicitly.

W2. Derive the \(2\times 2\) formula. Let \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} \] with \(ad-bc\neq 0\). Reduce \([A\mid I]\) to \([I\mid A^{-1}]\) (or clear systematically with elementary matrices) and show \[ A^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}. \] (We will call \(ad-bc\) the determinant on Friday.)

W3. Use the formula. Compute \(A^{-1}\) for \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{bmatrix} \] using W2. Check \(A^{-1}A=I\).

W4. Another \(2\times 2\). Find \(A^{-1}\) for \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix} \] by the formula, then check \(AA^{-1}=I\).

W5. \(3\times 3\) by elimination. Form \([A\mid I]\) for \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \] and reduce the left block to \(I\). Read \(A^{-1}\) from the right block. Check \(A^{-1}A=I\).

W6. No inverse. Try the same method for \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}. \] What goes wrong in \([A\mid I]\)? Connect to \(Ax=0\) or dependent columns.

W7. When does an inverse exist? For square \(A\), list equivalent ideas (any three):

  • can reduce \(A\) to \(I\) by elementary matrices
  • \(A^{-1}\) exists
  • \(Ax=b\) has a unique solution for every \(b\)
  • \(Ax=0\) has only the zero solution
  • columns of \(A\) are independent
  • (Friday) \(\det A\neq 0\)

W8. Same inverse, two right-hand sides. For \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}, \] form \(A^{-1}\) (formula). Then compute \(x=A^{-1}b\) for \[ b=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix} \quad\text{and}\quad b'=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}. \] Check \(Ax=b\) and \(Ax'=b'\). Same \(A^{-1}\), two different answers — that is the “general undo” idea.

Your turn

Y1. Using elementary matrices / \([A\mid I]\), find \(A^{-1}\) for \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] Check \(A^{-1}A=I\).

Y2. Use the \(2\times 2\) formula on \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix} \] (\(90^\circ\) CCW). What geometric map is \(A^{-1}\)?

Y3. Reduce \([A\mid I]\) for \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\2&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \] and read \(A^{-1}\). Check one of \(A^{-1}A\) or \(AA^{-1}\).

Y4. Does \[ A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix} \] have an inverse? Explain using \([A\mid I]\) or \(Ax=b\).

Y5. True or false, with a one-line reason: if \(A\) is invertible, then for every \(b\) the system \(Ax=b\) has exactly one solution.

Y6. True or false, with a one-line reason: if you already know \(A^{-1}\), you can solve \(Ax=b\) for a new \(b\) without redoing elimination on \(A\) — just multiply \(A^{-1}b\).

NoteAnswers (Your turn only)

Y1. Clear the \((1,2)\) entry: \(E=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(EA=I\), so \[ A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}. \] Check: \(A^{-1}A=I\).

Y2. \(ad-bc=1\), so \[ A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix} \] (rotation \(90^\circ\) clockwise). Check: \(A^{-1}A=I\).

Y3. \([A\mid I]\) reduces to right block \[ A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\-2&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \] (subtract \(2\) times row 1 from row 2 on the wide matrix). Check: \(A^{-1}A=I\).

Y4. No. Left block of \([A\mid I]\) gets a zero row; cannot reach \(I\). Equivalently \(Ax=(0,1)\) has no solution, or \(Ax=0\) has nontrivial solutions such as \((0,1)\).

Y5. True. Invertibility means \(x=A^{-1}b\) is the unique solution for every \(b\).

Y6. True. That is the point of \(A^{-1}\): one general undo for every \(b\).

## Fill in

### Inverse from elementary matrices

A square matrix $A$ is **invertible** if there exist elementary matrices $E_1,\ldots,E_k$ (row operations) with
$$
E_k\cdots E_2 E_1 A = \underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}.
$$

$$
A^{-1} := E_k\cdots E_2 E_1.
$$

$$
