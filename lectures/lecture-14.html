Lecture 14 — Basis, dimension, and orthonormal vectors

Mon Sep 28

Modified

September 22, 2026

Download PDF

Last updated: September 22, 2026.

Reading: Boyd 5.2–5.3 (basis, orthonormal vectors). Related objectives: Lectures. Schedule. No quiz today — Midterm 1 is Friday, October 2.

Bring paper. Warm-up while attendance is taken, then fill-in notes, board examples, and Check yourself. Gram–Schmidt is a short application at the end if time.

Learning objectives

By the end of class, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what a basis accomplishes.
  2. Identify a basis and dimension in simple examples.
  3. Use linear combination, span, subspace, independence, basis, and dimension accurately.
  4. Recognize an orthonormal collection.
  5. Explain why orthonormal coordinates are convenient for measurement and reconstruction.

Warm-up

Let \(a=(1,0)\) and \(b=(1,1)\).

  1. Is \(\{a,b\}\) independent? Does it span \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

  2. Can every \(v=(v_1,v_2)\) be written uniquely as \(\alpha a+\beta b\)? (Try \(v=(2,3)\).)

Fill in

A basis of a subspace \(V\) is a list of vectors that is both

  1. \(\underline{\hspace{2.8cm}}\) (no redundancy), and
  2. a \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\) set for \(V\) (every vector in \(V\) is a linear combination of the list).

What a basis accomplishes: every \(v\in V\) has coefficients (coordinates) with respect to the basis — a unique recipe \(v=\beta_1 a_1+\cdots+\beta_k a_k\).

The dimension of \(V\) is the number of vectors in \(\underline{\hspace{2.4cm}}\) of \(V\). (All bases of \(V\) have the same length.)

Examples:

  • \(\dim\mathbb{R}^n=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\) (standard basis \(e_1,\ldots,e_n\)).
  • A line through the origin through a nonzero \(a\) has dimension \(\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\).
  • A plane through the origin spanned by two independent vectors has dimension \(\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\).

An orthonormal list \(q_1,\ldots,q_k\) satisfies \(q_i^{\mathsf{T}}q_j=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\) if \(i\neq j\), and \(\lVert q_i\rVert=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\).

For an orthonormal basis of a subspace, the coefficients of \(v\) are especially simple: \[ \beta_i = \underline{\hspace{2.4cm}} \] (inner products — no linear system to solve). That is why orthonormal coordinates are convenient for measurement and reconstruction.

Worked in class

W1. Basis or not? For each list, is it a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^2\)? If not, say whether it fails independence, spanning, or both.

  1. \((1,0),\ (0,1)\)

  2. \((1,1),\ (2,2)\)

  3. \((1,0),\ (0,1),\ (1,1)\)

  4. \((1,1)\) alone

W2. Dimension. Let \(V=\operatorname{span}\{(1,2,0),(2,4,0),(0,0,1)\}\). Find a basis of \(V\) and \(\dim V\).

W3. Vocabulary drill. Fill each blank with one of: linear combination, span, subspace, independent, dependent, basis, dimension.

  1. \(\operatorname{span}\{a,b\}\) is always a \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

  2. If \(c=2a-b\), then \(\{a,b,c\}\) is \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\).

  3. A \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\) of \(V\) is an independent spanning list for \(V\).

W4. Orthonormal check. Which of these pairs in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is orthonormal?

  1. \((1,0),\ (0,1)\)

  2. \((1,1)/\sqrt{2},\ (1,-1)/\sqrt{2}\)

  3. \((1,0),\ (1,1)\)

W5. Orthonormal coordinates. Let \(q_1=(1,0)\) and \(q_2=(0,1)\) (standard basis — already orthonormal). For \(v=(3,-2)\), what are \(\beta_1=q_1^{\mathsf{T}}v\) and \(\beta_2=q_2^{\mathsf{T}}v\)? Check \(v=\beta_1 q_1+\beta_2 q_2\).

W6 (time permitting). Gram–Schmidt idea. From independent \(a_1=(1,1)\) and \(a_2=(1,0)\), form \(q_1=a_1/\lVert a_1\rVert\), then subtract from \(a_2\) its projection onto \(q_1\), and normalize. (Board sketch only.)

Check yourself

C1. A basis of a subspace is a list that is \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\) and \(\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\).

C2. \(\dim\operatorname{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}=\underline{\hspace{1.0cm}}\).

C3. True or false: every spanning list for \(V\) is a basis of \(V\).

C4. If \(\{q_1,q_2\}\) is orthonormal and \(v=\beta_1 q_1+\beta_2 q_2\), then \(\beta_1=\underline{\hspace{2.0cm}}\).

C5. Why are orthonormal coordinates nicer than coordinates in a skewed basis like \((1,0),\ (1,1)\)?

NoteAnswers (Check yourself only)

C1. independent and spanning (for that subspace).

C2. \(2\)

C3. False. Spanning lists can be dependent (too many vectors); a basis cannot.

C4. \(\beta_1=q_1^{\mathsf{T}}v\)

C5. Coefficients are inner products (no \(2\times 2\) solve); lengths and angles stay easy because the basis vectors are unit and orthogonal.
